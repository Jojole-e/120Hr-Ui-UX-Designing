01
What is UI/UX? How the industry works
Roles, deliverables, tools, and how to think like a designer from day one
5 hrs
▼
📖 Mini Read — what you need to know

UI (User Interface) is what a product looks like — buttons, colors, typography, layout. Think of it as the visual layer.

UX (User Experience) is how a product feels to use — is it easy? Does it make sense? Does it solve the user's problem? Think of it as the logic layer.

A UI designer makes it look good. A UX designer makes it work well. Most jobs need both skills. Your goal: learn both.

The design process follows the Double Diamond: Discover → Define → Develop → Deliver. You'll use this pattern for every project in this roadmap.

Visual Design Principles
Contrast, alignment, proximity, hierarchy — the rules every good design follows
5 hrs
▼
📖 Mini Read

There are 6 principles that govern almost every design decision you'll ever make. They aren't rules you memorise — they're things your eye already knows, but your brain hasn't named yet.

Contrast makes things stand out. Alignment creates order. Proximity groups related items. Repetition creates consistency. Hierarchy tells the eye where to look first. White space gives elements room to breathe.

Open any app right now. Every design decision you see is one of these 6 principles at work.

03
Typography & Color Theory
Type scales, font pairing, color systems, and accessibility contrast
5 hrs
▼
📖 Mini Read

Typography is one of the most powerful tools in design. 90% of UI is text. Bad typography makes even beautiful layouts feel off. The key things to control: font choice, size scale, line height, and weight contrast.

Color works through relationships, not individual colors. The most reliable system: pick one primary color, one accent color, and build everything else in neutrals (whites, grays, blacks).

Always check contrast ratios. The WCAG AA standard requires a 4.5:1 ratio for body text. If someone with low vision can't read your text, your design fails.

07
Grids, Spacing & Layout Systems
The 8pt grid, column grids, and responsive breakpoints
5 hrs
▼
📖 Mini Read

The 8pt grid means every spacing value in your design is a multiple of 8 (8, 16, 24, 32, 48…). This creates visual harmony without you having to think about it. Almost every major tech company uses this: Google, Apple, Airbnb, Spotify.

Column grids divide your canvas into columns (usually 4 on mobile, 12 on desktop) with gutters between them. Content lives in the columns; breathing room lives in the gutters.

Consistent spacing is what separates designs that "feel right" from designs that feel off — even when the viewer can't say why.

Mobile App UI Patterns
Navigation bars, tab bars, lists, cards, sheets, and modals
5 hrs
▼
📖 Mini Read

A UI pattern is a reusable solution to a common design problem. When users see a tab bar at the bottom of a screen, they already know it switches between sections — because every app uses it the same way. Don't reinvent patterns that already work.

The most important patterns to master: Tab bar navigation (bottom, 3–5 tabs), Cards (contain related info), Bottom sheets (slide up from bottom for actions), Modals (overlay for important decisions), and Empty states (what shows when there's no content yet).

Prototyping & Micro-interactions
Clickable prototypes, transitions, smart animate, and hover states
5 hrs
▼
📖 Mini Read

A prototype is a clickable version of your design — not real code, but it feels real enough to test with users. The goal is to simulate the experience without building it.

Micro-interactions are the tiny animations that make an interface feel alive — a button that squishes when pressed, a toggle that slides smoothly, a screen that slides in from the right. They communicate state changes and make digital interfaces feel physical.

In Figma, use Smart Animate between frames that share matching layer names. Figma interpolates between states automatically — it's powerful.

Web UI — Landing Pages & Dashboards
Hero sections, navigation, cards, tables, data visualization layouts
5 hrs
▼
📖 Mini Read

Web UI follows different rules than mobile. More screen real estate means more options — and more chances to confuse users. The best web designs resist that temptation and stay focused.

A great landing page answers three questions above the fold: What is this? Who is it for? What should I do next? The hero section — the first thing you see — must answer all three in under 5 seconds.

Dashboards are about information density and hierarchy. Users scan, not read. Use cards, big numbers, and clear labels. Every piece of data needs a label — never make users guess what a number means.

Accessibility & Inclusive Design
Contrast, touch targets, readable text, and designing for everyone
5 hrs
▼
📖 Mini Read

Accessibility means designing so that people with disabilities can use your product. 1 in 4 adults in the world has some form of disability — visual, motor, cognitive, or auditory. Inaccessible design excludes them.

The good news: accessible design is almost always better design for everyone. High contrast, larger touch targets, and clear labels improve the experience for every single user, not just those with disabilities.

The three most important things you can control as a beginner: Color contrast (4.5:1 minimum for text), Touch target size (minimum 44x44pt on iOS / 48x48dp on Android), and Focus states (visible keyboard navigation for everyone who doesn't use a mouse).

13
User Research Fundamentals
Interviews, surveys, personas, empathy maps, and research synthesis
5 hrs
▼
📖 Mini Read

Most bad products weren't built by bad designers — they were built by designers who never talked to users. User research is the practice of finding out what real people actually need, not what you assume they need.

There are two types of research: Generative (discovering what problems exist — you do this before designing) and Evaluative (testing whether your design solves those problems — you do this after designing).

The most powerful method for beginners: a 5-person user interview. Research shows that 5 interviews reveal 85% of major usability problems. You don't need 50 people — you need 5 honest conversations.

Generative vs evaluative research
How to write interview questions that aren't leading

Personas, User Journeys & Problem Statements
Turn research into design direction: personas, journey maps, "How Might We"
5 hrs
▼
📖 Mini Read

A persona is a fictional but research-based character that represents a group of real users. It keeps your team focused: "Would Sarah (our persona) understand this?" is a better question than "Does this look good?"

A journey map traces what a user does, thinks, and feels from the moment they discover your product to achieving their goal. Pain points show up as valleys on the map — and those valleys become your design opportunities.

A "How Might We" (HMW) statement frames a problem as a design challenge. "How might we help Sarah track her spending without feeling anxious?" is better than "Make budgeting less scary" — it opens space for creative solutions.

Information Architecture & User Flows
Site maps, user flows, task flows — how to structure complex apps
5 hrs
▼
📖 Mini Read

Information architecture (IA) is the skeleton of your product — how content is organised, labelled, and connected. Good IA is invisible. Bad IA makes users feel lost.

A site map shows the hierarchy of all screens (like a family tree for your app). A user flow shows the path a user takes to complete a specific task — including decision points and alternate paths.

Before you wireframe a single screen, draw the flow. If the flow makes sense, the screens will make sense. If the flow is confusing, no amount of beautiful screens will save it.

Wireframing — the Fast Way to Test Ideas
Lo-fi wireframes, sketching, rapid iteration, and getting feedback early
5 hrs
▼
📖 Mini Read

A wireframe is a rough, black-and-white layout of a screen. No color, no fancy typography, no images. Just boxes and labels that show where things will go.

Wireframes serve one purpose: testing ideas cheaply before you invest time in high-fidelity design. It takes 10 minutes to sketch a wireframe and 3 hours to design the same screen in full color. Catch problems at the wireframe stage.

Start on paper. Paper wireframes take 5 minutes and nobody gets attached to them. When the paper version makes sense, move to Figma for mid-fidelity wireframes (grayscale, proper spacing, but still no color or imagery).

Usability Testing
Moderated testing, task scripts, think-aloud protocol, and synthesising findings
5 hrs
▼
📖 Mini Read

Usability testing is watching real people try to use your design. You don't ask "do you like it?" — you give them a task and observe what happens. Where they hesitate, click wrong, or give up tells you everything.

The think-aloud protocol asks participants to narrate their thoughts while using the product: "I'm looking for the checkout button… I'm not sure what this icon does…" This surfaces hidden confusion that you'd never see by looking at analytics.

You only need 5 participants to find 85% of usability problems. Don't wait for a perfect prototype or a big budget. Test early, test often, test with a friend if you have to.

19
Writing Your First Case Study
What to include, how to structure it, and what hiring managers want to see
5 hrs
▼
📖 Mini Read

A case study is the story of a design project — not a gallery of pretty screenshots. Hiring managers want to see how you think, not just what you made.

Every case study must answer 4 questions: What was the problem? (context + user research) What did you explore? (wireframes, options considered) What did you decide and why? (the design + your reasoning) What happened? (results, learnings, what you'd do differently).

Write for a reader who has 90 seconds. Lead with the problem and the result. Put the process in the middle. Keep it honest — admitting what didn't work shows more maturity than pretending everything went perfectly.

Your portfolio is your most important design project. Use Framer (free, no-code, looks stunning) or Notion + Super.so (simpler, good for writing-heavy portfolios). Avoid Wix and Squarespace — they signal "I don't know design tools."

Structure: Home → 2 case studies → About → Contact. That's it. Don't overthink it. The work should speak; the site is just the container.

Your homepage needs: your name, one sentence about what you do, and your best project first. If a recruiter can't find your work in 3 seconds, they'll leave.

Figma Advanced + Dev Handoff
Variables, dark mode, Dev Mode, and working with developers
5 hrs
▼
📖 Mini Read

When your design moves to development, engineers need specific information: exact sizes, colors (in hex/rgb), spacing values, component states, and interaction specs. Dev Mode in Figma (free) gives them all of this automatically when your file is well-organised.

Variables (Figma's version of design tokens) let you create a dark mode version of your entire design by changing one setting. This is how professional design teams build scalable systems.
